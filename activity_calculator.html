Decay physics reference
α Alpha decay
Emission of a helium-4 nucleus (2p + 2n). Reduces A by 4, Z by 2. Short range in matter (~5 cm in air), easily stopped by paper or skin. Highly ionising — dangerous if inhaled or ingested. Common in heavy nuclei (Z > 82).
β⁻ Beta minus decay
A neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and antineutrino. Z increases by 1, A unchanged. Penetrating — stopped by a few mm of aluminium. Energy is shared between electron and antineutrino, giving a continuous spectrum.
β⁺ Positron decay
A proton becomes a neutron, emitting a positron and neutrino. Z decreases by 1. The positron annihilates with an electron, producing two 511 keV gamma photons in opposite directions — the basis of PET imaging.
γ Gamma emission
High-energy photon emitted when a nucleus relaxes from an excited state. No change in A or Z. Most penetrating — requires lead or thick concrete for shielding. Useful for external imaging (SPECT, scintigraphy) and radiotherapy.
Half-life
The time for exactly half of a radioactive sample to decay. It is a fundamental property of each nuclide and is unaffected by temperature, pressure, or chemical state. After n half-lives, the fraction remaining is (½)ⁿ.
Activity & units
Activity (A) is the rate of decay events per second. 1 Becquerel (Bq) = 1 decay/s. 1 Curie (Ci) = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq. 1 MBq = 10⁶ Bq. Activity decays exponentially: A(t) = A₀ · e⁻λt where λ = ln2 / t½.
